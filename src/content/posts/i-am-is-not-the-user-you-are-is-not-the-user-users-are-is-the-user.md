# "I Am" Is Not the User. "You Are" Is Not the User. "Users Are" Is the User.

One of the most subtle mistakes in product design often starts with a single word.

"I."

Designers think, "I would use it this way."

Product managers think, "I prefer this workflow."

Developers think, "This makes sense to me."

Stakeholders think, "I like this version better."

And that's where many user experience problems begin.

The moment decisions are based on what we think, rather than what users need, the design process starts drifting away from its purpose.

UX has never been about designing for ourselves.

It has always been about designing for others.

## The Danger of "I Am"

Experience can be valuable. Expertise matters.

But expertise can also create blind spots.

The more familiar teams become with a product, the less likely they are to notice the friction that first-time users experience.

What feels obvious to the team may be confusing to someone seeing the interface for the first time.

What feels efficient internally may feel overwhelming externally.

The challenge is simple: product teams know too much.

Users don't.

That gap often explains why products that look perfect in internal reviews struggle in the real world.

## The Problem with "You Are"

Even user-centered conversations sometimes fall into another trap.

Design discussions frequently begin with phrases like:

"You want to click here."

"You understand what this button means."

"You will know what happens next."

The intention is good, but the assumption is dangerous.

There is no single "you."

Users are not a homogeneous group.

They come from different backgrounds, different industries, different age groups, different levels of technical expertise, and different accessibility needs.

What seems intuitive to one person may be completely unclear to another.

Designing for an imagined individual often creates experiences that work well for some users while excluding others.

## The Shift That Changes Everything: "Users Are"

Great UX begins when the conversation changes.

Instead of asking:

"What would I do?"

The question becomes:

"What are users trying to accomplish?"

Instead of saying:

"You should know this."

The focus becomes:

"How can this be made clear for different types of users?"

This subtle shift changes how products are researched, designed, tested, and improved.

It moves the discussion away from opinions and toward evidence.

Away from assumptions and toward understanding.

Away from personal preferences and toward user outcomes.

## Designing for Real Behaviors

Users rarely behave the way teams expect them to.

They skip instructions.

They overlook important information.

They use products in noisy environments, poor lighting conditions, on slow networks, and while multitasking.

They arrive with different goals, different levels of motivation, and varying degrees of patience.

The most successful products are not designed for ideal scenarios.

They are designed for real-world behavior.

That is why research matters.

That is why usability testing matters.

That is why analytics, feedback, accessibility reviews, and continuous iteration matter.

Every one of these activities helps teams understand what users actually do rather than what they are expected to do.

## Empathy Is More Than a UX Buzzword

Empathy is often described as the ability to understand another person's perspective.

In UX, it means recognizing that users do not share the team's knowledge, assumptions, priorities, or context.

It means accepting that confusion is not a user problem.

It is a design problem.

When users struggle, the goal should not be to teach them how the product works.

The goal should be to improve the product so it works the way users expect.

The responsibility belongs to the experience, not the user.

## The Best Products Are Built Around Users

The strongest products rarely emerge from personal opinions.

They emerge from observation.

From listening.

From testing.

From learning.

And most importantly, from accepting that the people building the product are not the people using it.

Technology continues to evolve.

AI is changing how products are conceived, built, and delivered.

Yet one principle remains unchanged:

The product is not about the designer.

It is not about the stakeholder.

It is not about the developer.

It is not even about the technology.

It is about the people who use it.

Because in UX, "I am" is not the user.

"You are" is not the user.

"Users are" the user.
