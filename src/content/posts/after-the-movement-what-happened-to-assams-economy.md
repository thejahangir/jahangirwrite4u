# After the Movement: What Happened to Assam’s Economy?

The Assam Movement was one of the defining political movements of post-Independence India. Between 1979 and 1985, it transformed the political consciousness of an entire generation and eventually culminated in the Assam Accord, signed on 15 August 1985 between the Government of India, the Government of Assam and the movement's leading organisations. The movement had emerged around questions of identity, immigration, electoral legitimacy and the protection of Assamese political and cultural interests. But beneath those questions was another anxiety that is sometimes less prominently discussed: the anxiety of economic decline. Assam had resources, but much of its population remained poor. It produced oil, tea, timber and agricultural commodities, yet industrial development remained weak. The state possessed enormous natural wealth but struggled to convert that wealth into broad-based prosperity. The Assam Accord therefore contained an economic promise as well as a political one. Clause 7 explicitly renewed the government's commitment to the “speedy all round economic development of Assam,” with particular emphasis on education, science and technology and national institutions.

The question, four decades later, is not whether Assam has developed. It clearly has. The more difficult question is whether the economic transformation after 1985 was proportional to the expectations created by the Movement and the Accord. That answer is much less comfortable. Assam today has better roads, bridges, educational institutions, refineries, telecommunications, urban infrastructure and connectivity than it did in the 1980s. Yet the fundamental problem that haunted the pre-Movement economy—how to convert Assam's natural and human resources into sustained, diversified, high-productivity employment—has remained remarkably persistent.

To understand why, we have to go back even further. Assam's economic problem did not begin with the Assam Movement. In the early decades after Independence, the state gradually fell behind the national economy in per-capita income. Historical data show that Assam's per-capita income had already fallen below the national average by the 1960s, after having been comparatively stronger earlier. By the 1980s, the gap had become a serious structural problem. Data compiled in government economic studies show that Assam's real per-capita income remained substantially below the Indian average through the 1980s and early 1990s. The Movement therefore emerged in a society already experiencing economic frustration. Identity politics did not exist separately from economic anxiety; the two reinforced one another.

This is important because it changes the way the economic history of the Assam Movement should be interpreted. It is tempting to imagine that the Movement ended in 1985 and that Assam then entered a new economic chapter. In reality, the political settlement did not automatically resolve the structural weaknesses that had contributed to the sense of deprivation. The Assam Accord could establish commitments. It could not, by itself, create factories, entrepreneurs, skilled workers, reliable logistics or competitive markets.

And then came one of the greatest obstacles to economic transformation: prolonged insurgency and political instability.

The years following the Accord were not an uninterrupted period of economic peace. Assam experienced insurgency, counter-insurgency, political violence, extortion, disruption and uncertainty. These conditions mattered economically because investment is highly sensitive to uncertainty. A factory is not merely a building and machinery; it is a long-term bet on electricity, transport, labour, markets, security, regulation and political stability. When the future appears unpredictable, private investment becomes more difficult to attract.

The economic consequences were particularly visible in employment. World Bank research on conflict in Assam found that youth unemployment increased dramatically during the conflict period; between 1983-84 and 1993-94, Assam experienced one of the sharpest increases in youth unemployment among major Indian states, with the rate by the latter period around three times the all-India level. That is an important piece of the post-Movement story because unemployment was not simply an economic statistic. It created a generation of young people with limited opportunities at precisely the time when Assam was experiencing political instability.

This produced a vicious circle. Political instability discouraged investment. Weak investment limited employment. Limited employment created frustration. Frustration contributed to political instability. The economy became trapped in a cycle in which the absence of economic opportunity reinforced the political conditions that made economic investment more difficult.

Yet it would be unfair to describe the post-1985 period simply as economic failure. One of the most important achievements of the decades after the Accord was the expansion of national institutions and strategic infrastructure. IIT Guwahati, universities, cultural institutions, the Numaligarh Refinery, the Brahmaputra Gas Cracker project and major bridges such as Bogibeel emerged as part of the broader development trajectory associated with the Accord and subsequent central and state initiatives. The Government of Assam itself lists these among the tangible achievements associated with implementation of the Accord.

But here lies an important distinction: building economic infrastructure is not the same thing as building an economic ecosystem.

A refinery can create industrial value.

A bridge can reduce travel time.

A university can create human capital.

A highway can improve connectivity.

But none of these automatically creates a diversified private-sector economy.

Assam's difficulty has often been precisely this missing middle.

The state could attract a large project, but struggled to create the dense network of suppliers, manufacturers, service companies, logistics providers, technology businesses and skilled labour that turns individual projects into an industrial ecosystem.

This is why Assam's industrial history after 1985 is best understood as a series of islands of economic activity rather than a continuous industrial landscape.

Oil is one island.

Tea is another.

Petrochemicals another.

Government services another.

Construction another.

Tourism another.

Small enterprises another.

The missing piece has been the connective tissue between them.

This problem was recognized by policymakers. The 1997 North East Industrial Policy attempted to address the region's industrial disadvantages through infrastructure support, transport subsidies, tax incentives and investment subsidies. The policy explicitly acknowledged the region's continuing industrial backwardness and attempted to compensate for its structural disadvantages. Assam subsequently developed its own industrial policies and investment incentives, and the state continues to maintain policies covering investment, logistics, startups, electronics, ethanol, bamboo, warehousing and other sectors.

But incentives alone cannot manufacture an industrial culture.

A company does not invest only because taxes are lower. It invests because it believes it can make money.

That requires customers, suppliers, skilled workers, infrastructure, financing, predictable regulation, transport connectivity and confidence that the investment will remain viable for decades.

Assam has historically struggled with several of these simultaneously.

Geography is part of the explanation, but geography should not become an excuse. Assam is distant from India's major western and southern industrial clusters, and much of the Northeast is landlocked. Transportation costs have historically been significant. Yet geography can be overcome when infrastructure and markets compensate for it. The more interesting question is why Assam has not converted its strategic position into a sufficiently powerful advantage.

This is particularly relevant because Assam is effectively the gateway to the Northeast. World Bank analysis has described the state as the gateway to the landlocked northeastern region and highlighted its potential in hydropower, agriculture, tourism and forestry, while also noting inadequate infrastructure and market access as constraints.

That creates an enormous opportunity.

Assam does not necessarily need to become another Gujarat, Maharashtra or Tamil Nadu.

It needs to become the economic platform of the Northeast.

That could mean logistics, warehousing, food processing, healthcare, education, tourism, regional distribution, aviation, digital services, agro-processing and manufacturing connected to the surrounding northeastern states.

But this requires thinking beyond Assam's internal market.

The state has a relatively limited domestic market compared with India's largest states. Its economic future therefore depends partly on its ability to serve markets outside Assam.

This is where the Brahmaputra, railways, highways, airports and neighbouring countries become economically significant. Assam's geography places it close to Bangladesh and at the centre of the northeastern region. If transportation and logistics costs can be reduced sufficiently, Assam can convert geographical centrality into economic centrality.

But that opportunity has repeatedly arrived before the institutions required to exploit it.

This is perhaps one of the central themes of Assam's post-Movement economic history: the state has often possessed strategic advantages before it has possessed the institutional capacity to monetize them.

Consider natural resources.

Assam has oil.

Assam has natural gas.

Assam has tea.

Assam has forests.

Assam has fertile agricultural land.

Assam has enormous water resources.

Assam has biodiversity.

Assam has tourism potential.

Assam has a young population.

Assam has a strategic geographical position.

The question has never really been whether Assam has resources.

The question has been how much value is created in Assam from those resources.

That distinction between extraction and value creation is critical.

Tea can be grown in Assam but processed, branded, marketed and consumed elsewhere.

Oil can be extracted from Assam while much of the higher-value industrial ecosystem develops elsewhere.

Agricultural commodities can leave Assam in relatively low-value forms while processed products return at higher prices.

Natural resources can generate government revenue without creating enough private-sector employment.

This is the difference between being a resource-rich economy and becoming a resource-based industrial economy.

Assam has often been closer to the former than the latter.

The story of tea illustrates the problem particularly well. Assam is globally associated with tea, yet tea's economic importance does not automatically translate into broad-based prosperity for the wider population. A plantation economy can be productive without creating a sufficiently diversified regional economy. The crucial question is what happens around the plantation: processing, packaging, branding, research, machinery, logistics, tourism, hospitality and downstream businesses.

The same logic applies to oil and gas.

The real opportunity is not simply extracting hydrocarbons.

It is building petrochemical, chemical, engineering, logistics and manufacturing ecosystems around them.

The establishment of Numaligarh Refinery was therefore significant, but a refinery alone cannot transform an economy. The economic multiplier comes when downstream industries, suppliers, skilled workers and supporting services develop around it. The challenge for Assam has repeatedly been to move from individual industrial assets to industrial clusters.

This problem is not unique to Assam, but it has been particularly important because the state's natural-resource base created the illusion that industrialization would happen automatically.

It did not.

Resources create possibilities.

Institutions convert possibilities into economic value.

The fiscal structure of the state has also mattered. Assam, like several northeastern states, has historically depended significantly on transfers and public expenditure. Public investment has therefore played an important role in sustaining the economy. This is not inherently negative; government expenditure can be an essential instrument for developing infrastructure and human capital. The problem emerges when public expenditure becomes the dominant engine without sufficient private-sector expansion.

A government can build a road.

It cannot permanently substitute for thousands of competitive businesses using that road.

A government can establish a university.

It cannot by itself create an innovation ecosystem around every graduate.

A government can provide subsidies.

It cannot guarantee that a subsidized company will become globally competitive.

The challenge is to use public investment as a platform for private economic activity rather than as a permanent substitute for it.

This is where Assam's post-2000 trajectory becomes particularly interesting. World Bank analysis noted that Assam's economic and fiscal situation improved after 2004, with the state's development strategy helping contain fiscal and revenue deficits and GSDP growth accelerating from around 2.5 percent during 1991-2001 to 5.2 percent during 2001-2010. At the same time, the analysis noted that the state continued to struggle with industrial diversification.

That is an important transition.

It suggests that the post-Movement story is not simply one of stagnation.

Assam did improve.

But improvement and transformation are not the same thing.

The state moved forward without completely changing the structure of its economy.

This distinction is often lost in political debates because economic arguments are presented as binary choices: Assam developed or Assam did not develop. The reality is more complicated. Assam experienced periods of progress, but the progress was uneven and insufficiently transformative to eliminate the structural gap.

The emergence of Guwahati as a regional urban and commercial centre is perhaps one of the clearest examples of this partial transformation. Guwahati increasingly became the financial, educational, medical, retail, hospitality, transportation and administrative centre of the Northeast. But the concentration of economic activity in one metropolitan area also reveals another problem: Assam's economic development has not always spread evenly across the state.

A strong Guwahati does not automatically mean a strong Assam.

The state needs secondary cities and towns capable of developing their own economic specializations.

Dibrugarh can build around energy, education and healthcare.

Jorhat can build around agriculture, education, research and culture.

Silchar can become a gateway for Barak Valley and neighbouring markets.

Tinsukia can strengthen logistics and energy-related industries.

Tezpur can develop education, tourism and services.

Smaller towns can become specialized economic nodes.

The future cannot depend entirely on one city absorbing everyone else's economic aspirations.

There is another uncomfortable legacy of the post-Movement period: migration.

Large numbers of young Assamese have historically looked outside the state for higher education and employment. Some leave for Delhi, Bengaluru, Mumbai, Hyderabad, Chennai, Pune or abroad. Migration itself is not necessarily a failure. In a modern economy, people move toward opportunity. Remittances and returning skills can benefit the home state.

The problem arises when a state consistently exports its ambitious young people because it cannot provide comparable opportunities locally.

Then migration becomes an indicator of economic weakness.

A young person leaving Assam to become a software engineer in Bengaluru is not a problem.

A young person leaving Assam because there is no meaningful professional ecosystem in which to build a career is a problem.

The distinction is important.

The objective should not be to prevent Assamese people from leaving.

It should be to create enough opportunities that leaving becomes a choice rather than a necessity.

This is where education becomes central.

Clause 7 of the Assam Accord explicitly connected economic development with education, science and technology and national institutions. The establishment of IIT Guwahati and other higher-education institutions represented important progress. But the economic return from education depends on whether the surrounding economy can absorb and reward skilled people.

An educated population without a sufficiently diversified economy creates migration.

A diversified economy without skilled people creates labour shortages.

The two must grow together.

This is perhaps why the post-Movement economic challenge cannot be reduced to “more industries.” Assam needs a complete economic ecosystem: education, skills, finance, infrastructure, entrepreneurship, technology, logistics, urbanization and markets.

The next phase of Assam's development should therefore be less about asking how many projects have been announced and more about asking how many productive enterprises have survived, expanded and created employment.

Project announcements are politically visible.

Business survival is economically meaningful.

Investment MoUs are easy to count.

Operating factories are harder.

Road kilometres are measurable.

The number of businesses that become globally competitive is a more revealing metric.

This is particularly important in the current investment climate, where Assam is actively seeking large-scale investment through initiatives such as Advantage Assam and newer sectoral policies. The state now has policies covering investment, logistics, startups, electronics and other emerging sectors. The question should not be whether Assam can attract headlines about investment.

The question is whether it can convert investment commitments into durable economic capacity.

That requires a different kind of governance.

Investors do not need only incentives.

They need predictability.

Entrepreneurs do not need only subsidies.

They need markets.

Workers do not need only training certificates.

They need jobs.

Universities do not need only buildings.

They need research ecosystems.

Cities do not need only flyovers.

They need productive economic functions.

And industries do not need only land.

They need suppliers, customers, talent and logistics.

The post-Assam Movement generation therefore faces a fundamentally different challenge from the generation that launched the Movement.

The earlier generation asked, “Who are we?”

The next generation has to ask, “What economy can we build?”

That does not make identity irrelevant. In fact, cultural confidence and economic confidence can reinforce each other. But a society cannot indefinitely sustain itself on political identity alone. Identity can protect culture; it cannot substitute for productivity.

A state cannot create prosperity merely by defending its identity.

It must create institutions that allow its people to prosper.

That may be the deepest unresolved economic question inherited from the Assam Movement.

The Movement successfully transformed the political conversation around Assamese identity and citizenship. The Accord created commitments around safeguards and economic development. The decades that followed produced substantial infrastructure and institutional development. Yet the economic transformation remained incomplete because the state never fully escaped the combination of low industrial diversification, weak private-sector depth, geographic disadvantages, conflict-related uncertainty, recurring floods and erosion, limited urban economic centres and dependence on public expenditure.

None of this means that Assam failed.

It means Assam did not yet finish the transition.

The next chapter therefore needs a different ambition.

Assam should not simply seek compensation for historical disadvantages.

It should seek competitive advantages.

It should not merely ask the Centre for more assistance.

It should ask what it can build with that assistance.

It should not merely extract resources.

It should create value around them.

It should not merely send educated young people elsewhere.

It should create reasons for some of them to return.

It should not simply celebrate connectivity.

It should turn connectivity into commerce.

It should not simply protect the Brahmaputra from economic disruption.

It should build an economy capable of living productively with the river.

And perhaps most importantly, it should stop measuring development primarily by government spending and infrastructure announcements and begin measuring it by productivity, private investment, wages, enterprise creation, exports, innovation and the quality of employment.

The history after 1985 therefore leaves Assam with a paradox.

The Assam Movement was extraordinarily successful at creating political consciousness.

The Assam Accord created an institutional promise of economic development.

The following decades created important infrastructure and institutions.

But the central economic transformation—the transformation from a resource-rich but structurally weak economy into a diversified, high-productivity regional economic power—remains unfinished.

That is not necessarily a reason for pessimism.

It may actually be the most important reason for optimism.

Because Assam's fundamental problem is not the absence of economic assets.

It is the incomplete conversion of those assets into economic power.

The state already possesses many of the ingredients: natural resources, strategic geography, a major river system, tea, energy, agricultural potential, biodiversity, cultural capital, a growing urban centre and a large young population.

The unfinished task is to connect them.

The Assam Movement changed the political trajectory of the state.

Perhaps the next great transformation must change its economic trajectory.

And the defining question for the next generation may no longer be whether Assam can protect what it has.

It may be whether Assam can finally create enough economic value from what it already possesses.

The Assam Movement gave the state a powerful political awakening. The challenge now is to turn that awakening into an economic renaissance.
