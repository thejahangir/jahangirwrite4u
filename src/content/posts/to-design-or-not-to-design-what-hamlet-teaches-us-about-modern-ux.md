# To Design or Not to Design: What Hamlet Teaches Us About Modern UX

There is something strangely familiar about Hamlet to anyone who has spent years working in UX.

A prince stands in the middle of a complicated system, surrounded by incomplete information, conflicting signals, hidden agendas, unreliable actors, emotional pressure, and a decision that could have enormous consequences. He has information, but not certainty. He has people around him, but he cannot completely trust them. He has a problem, but the obvious solution may create an even bigger problem.

And most importantly, he keeps asking the question every experienced UX designer eventually asks:

> "What is really going on here?"

William Shakespeare's *Hamlet* is usually discussed as a tragedy about revenge, madness, mortality, morality, and indecision. But underneath the tragedy is something remarkably relevant to modern UX: the problem of making decisions when you don't have complete information.

Hamlet lives in a world where almost nothing is what it appears to be. His uncle is a king who is also a murderer. His friends are also informants. His lover becomes part of a surveillance strategy. His apparent madness may be an act, but eventually even the boundary between acting and reality becomes unclear. The court is full of people watching other people. Everyone is collecting information, interpreting behavior, and trying to determine what is true.

It sounds surprisingly close to the world of modern digital products.

## Incomplete Information Is the Real Brief

A UX designer rarely gets a perfectly defined problem. Instead, someone walks into a meeting and says, "Users are complaining about the dashboard." Another stakeholder says, "We need more features." Analytics show that users are abandoning a workflow. Customer support says something completely different. The product manager has a hypothesis. Engineering has constraints. Business has a deadline.

And somewhere in the middle sits the UX designer, trying to understand what the actual problem is.

This is where Hamlet becomes interesting.

Hamlet's first problem is not really revenge. His first problem is trust. His father's ghost tells him that Claudius murdered him and asks him to seek revenge. But Hamlet does not immediately accept the information as unquestionable truth. He wonders whether the ghost is actually his father's spirit or something deceptive.

His problem is essentially an evidence problem:

> Can I trust the information I have received?

That uncertainty drives much of what follows.

A UX designer faces the same problem constantly.

- A stakeholder says, "Users hate this." Do you believe it?
- A research participant says, "I would definitely use this." Do you believe it?
- Analytics show that sixty percent of users abandon a page. Do you know why?
- Customer support receives hundreds of complaints. Does that automatically mean the interface is bad?

The answer to all of these questions is not necessarily. The information is a signal, not the truth.

This is one of the most important lessons Hamlet can teach UX designers:

> Don't confuse information with understanding.

Having more data doesn't automatically mean having more clarity. You can have interviews, analytics, surveys, recordings, heatmaps, support tickets, stakeholder opinions, competitor research, usability tests, and workshops and still misunderstand the problem.

Hamlet has information everywhere. What he doesn't have is certainty. Modern UX has exactly the same problem.

## Good UX Helps Us Manage Uncertainty

We often imagine UX research as a machine that turns questions into answers. In reality, research often produces more questions. You interview five users and discover three different interpretations of the same feature. You analyze analytics and discover an unexpected behavior. You run a usability test and discover that the problem isn't where you thought it was. You speak to customer support and find out that users have created workarounds that never appeared in your original research.

Good UX doesn't eliminate uncertainty.

Good UX helps us manage uncertainty intelligently.

That distinction matters because the objective of research isn't to reach some mythical point where we know everything. The objective is to reduce the uncertainty that actually matters to the decision we are trying to make.

## Hamlet's Play Is a Prototype

Hamlet's response to uncertainty is to investigate. He doesn't simply attack Claudius immediately. He creates a test. He arranges for a play to be performed that mirrors the alleged murder of his father and watches Claudius's reaction. He uses an artificial situation to generate evidence about a real situation.

The play-within-the-play becomes a kind of behavioral experiment. Hamlet wants to see whether Claudius reacts in a way that confirms his suspicion.

And suddenly we have one of the most recognizable concepts in modern UX: the prototype.

A prototype is not the final product. It is an experiment. It is a controlled way of asking:

> "What happens if we do this?"

A good prototype doesn't prove that your design is correct. It helps you discover whether your assumption survives contact with reality. This is why prototypes are so powerful. Instead of spending three months building something and then asking users whether they like it, you can create something rough, put it in front of people, observe what happens, and change direction.

Hamlet essentially creates a prototype of the crime. The difference is that his usability test has much higher stakes.

The lesson for designers is simple:

> When uncertainty is high, test your assumptions before committing to the solution.

## Don't Let Research Become a Way of Avoiding Responsibility

But Hamlet also teaches us something more uncomfortable. Sometimes we can become so obsessed with gathering evidence that we stop making decisions.

UX designers love research. We love more interviews, more usability tests, more analytics, more competitor analysis, more workshops, more personas, more journey maps, and more validation. All of these things can be useful. But there comes a point when research stops reducing uncertainty and starts becoming a way of avoiding responsibility.

You can always conduct another interview. You can always test another prototype. You can always collect more data. You can always ask another stakeholder. At some point, someone has to say:

> "We know enough to make a decision."

This is one of the differences between junior and senior UX thinking.

A junior designer may ask, "Do we have enough research?"

A senior designer asks:

> "What uncertainty actually matters to this decision?"

Those are very different questions.

You don't need perfect information to design. You need enough confidence for the level of risk involved. If you're deciding whether an icon should be twenty-four pixels or twenty-eight pixels, you probably don't need six weeks of research. If you're designing a financial transaction flow, a medical interface, or an enterprise workflow that could affect thousands of employees, the standard of evidence should be much higher.

Hamlet's tragedy comes partly from the fact that the consequences of his decisions are enormous. UX designers need to understand the same relationship between uncertainty and consequence.

The higher the cost of being wrong, the more carefully you should validate your assumptions.

## Appearance Versus Reality

This brings us to another fascinating connection between *Hamlet* and UX: appearance versus reality.

Few ideas are more central to *Hamlet*. The characters constantly perform versions of themselves. Hamlet pretends to be mad. Claudius presents himself as a legitimate and capable king while hiding his crime. Polonius spies on people while presenting himself as a wise counselor. Rosencrantz and Guildenstern appear to be Hamlet's friends while secretly gathering information for the king. The entire court becomes an environment where appearances cannot automatically be trusted.

Now think about modern interfaces.

A button can look clickable but not be clickable. A notification can look urgent but be irrelevant. A dashboard can look sophisticated but provide no useful decision-making information. An AI response can sound confident while being completely wrong. A beautiful interface can hide a terrible workflow. A simple interface can hide enormous complexity.

This is one of the biggest challenges in contemporary UX:

> What the interface communicates visually is not always what the system actually does.

And that creates a trust problem.

Users develop mental models based on what they see. If the interface suggests one thing and the system does another, trust begins to disappear. Imagine clicking "Save" and discovering that the system actually published your content. Imagine clicking "Delete" and discovering that the action cannot be undone. Imagine asking an AI assistant to "clean up my document" and discovering afterward that it changed content you never intended to change.

The interface may have looked simple. But the underlying reality was different.

Good UX therefore isn't just about making interfaces understandable.

It is about making the system's behavior match the user's mental model.

## Designing Interpretation in the Age of AI

This becomes even more important in the age of AI. Traditional interfaces expose controls. AI interfaces increasingly expose intentions. You tell the system what you want, and the system decides how to achieve it. That creates an enormous gap between what the user asks for and what the system actually does.

A user might say, "Make this presentation better." What does "better" mean? More concise? More visual? More persuasive? More corporate? More modern? More accessible? The AI has to interpret the request. That means modern UX is increasingly becoming the design of interpretation.

The UX designer is no longer only designing buttons and navigation. The designer is designing how users communicate intent, how the system interprets that intent, how uncertainty is communicated, and how users regain control when the system gets it wrong.

In other words, modern UX has started to resemble *Hamlet* even more closely.

The system knows something. The user knows something. Neither has the complete picture.

The interface becomes the place where those two incomplete perspectives meet.

## Observation Without Understanding Can Become Surveillance

This brings us to another fascinating character in *Hamlet*: Polonius.

Polonius is obsessed with gathering information. He sends people to spy on Laertes. He spies on Hamlet. He listens from behind curtains. He believes that truth can be discovered through observation and indirect investigation. His approach is essentially to observe behavior and infer the underlying reality.

That sounds remarkably like UX research.

But Shakespeare gives us a warning.

Observation without understanding can become surveillance.

This distinction is increasingly important in modern product design. Just because we can collect behavioral data doesn't mean we should collect everything. Just because we can track every click doesn't mean every click tells us something meaningful. Just because an AI system can observe user behavior doesn't mean users should lose control over how that information is used.

The modern UX designer has to ask not only, "What can we measure?" but also, "What should we measure?"

And perhaps more importantly:

> "What right do we have to measure it?"

This is where ethics enters UX.

A product can technically be usable and still be harmful. A notification system can be extremely effective at bringing users back while intentionally exploiting attention. A subscription flow can be incredibly optimized while making cancellation difficult. An AI product can be remarkably convenient while hiding how decisions are being made.

A UX designer therefore needs another layer of thinking beyond usability:

> What is this design encouraging people to do?

Because design doesn't simply facilitate behavior.

Design shapes behavior.

## Designing for the Wrong Problem

Hamlet also offers another lesson: the danger of designing for the wrong problem.

Hamlet is told that revenge is his mission. But as the story develops, it becomes increasingly difficult to separate revenge from grief, morality, identity, political corruption, betrayal, and existential doubt. The apparent problem is "kill Claudius." The actual problem is vastly more complicated.

UX projects often behave the same way.

The initial brief says, "Redesign the dashboard." But after investigation, you discover that the real problem is information architecture. Or someone says, "Add AI search," but the actual problem is poor content organization. Someone says, "Improve onboarding," but the real problem is that users don't understand the product's value. Someone says, "Make the application faster," but the real problem is that users don't trust the result.

This is why the first problem statement should rarely be treated as the final problem statement.

> The brief is a starting hypothesis. Not the truth.

## Who's There?

Perhaps the most important lesson Hamlet gives modern UX designers is therefore not about interfaces at all. It is about asking better questions.

The play begins with a question: "Who's there?"

That is a remarkably appropriate question for UX.

- Who is the user?
- Who is actually making this decision?
- Who benefits from this feature?
- Who is excluded?
- Who is struggling?
- Who is responsible when something goes wrong?
- Who controls the system?
- Who has the information?
- Who doesn't?

And perhaps the most important question of all:

> Who are we designing for?

Because UX problems often become clearer when we stop asking, "What should we build?" and start asking:

> "Who is experiencing what problem, under what circumstances, and why?"

Hamlet spends much of the play searching for certainty. The UX designer has to learn something different.

Certainty is rarely available.

There will always be incomplete research. There will always be contradictory users. There will always be technical constraints. There will always be business pressure. There will always be edge cases. There will always be something you don't know.

The goal isn't to eliminate uncertainty.

The goal is to make responsible decisions despite uncertainty.

That might be the most modern UX lesson hidden inside Shakespeare's oldest questions.

## When You Know Enough to Act

A good UX designer investigates like Hamlet, but shouldn't become trapped in Hamlet's hesitation. A good UX designer observes like Polonius, but shouldn't turn observation into surveillance. A good UX designer understands the importance of appearance, but doesn't confuse visual polish with reality. A good UX designer uses prototypes like Hamlet's play-within-the-play not to prove they are right, but to discover what is true.

And a good UX designer understands that sometimes the most important question isn't, "What should we design?"

It is, "What do we actually know?"

And after that, "What don't we know?"

And finally, "What is the smallest, safest thing we can do to find out?"

That is where Shakespeare and UX unexpectedly meet.

*Hamlet* is a story about a person trying to make sense of an uncertain system. Modern UX is, in many ways, the same discipline. We observe. We question. We test. We interpret. We make decisions. We discover that our assumptions were wrong. We redesign. And then we do it all again.

The tragedy of Hamlet is that he spends too long searching for certainty in a world where certainty is impossible. The lesson for UX designers is almost the opposite:

> Don't wait for perfect certainty.

Understand the uncertainty.

Design the experiment.

Learn from the evidence.

Make the smallest responsible decision.

And keep moving.

Because in UX, as in *Hamlet*, the hardest question is rarely *what to do*.

The hardest question is knowing *when you know enough to act*.
